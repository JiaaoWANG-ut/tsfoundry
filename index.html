Transition-state search · invented by AI · verified

Find transition states from the initial state alone.

Transition states are the root of chemical reactivity: they decide which reaction happens and how fast. Transition Foundry unifies the mainstream transition-state algorithms and uses frontier AI models to autonomously invent new searches for first-order saddle points in the full 3N-dimensional configuration space. Every idea is tested on reactions it has never seen and kept only if it wins.

Problems it solves

The hard part of a reaction study is the transition state

Locating transition states on catalyst surfaces and in zeolite pores usually means guessing the product first, babysitting NEB bands and re-running dimer searches that wander off. Transition Foundry removes these bottlenecks.

⇢

No final state needed

NEB and CI-NEB need the product before they can start.

Give it the initial state only. Bond-coordinate relaxed scans propose where the reaction can go; the search finds the saddle point and the product for you.

⋔

A mechanism, not one path

A double-ended search returns one transition state for one guessed product.

One run returns every distinct certified saddle it finds around the initial state, with barriers, reaction energies and products, deduplicated and ranked: 7 in every run of the zeolite demo, 6.3 per run on average in the benchmark.

↯

Several times faster

Random-start dimer searches spend most of their effort on duplicates and failures.

4.2–6.2× more verified saddles per CPU-hour than ASE's dimer on 39 held-out reactions. The first verified saddle arrives after a median 9.5 CPU-s instead of 92 CPU-s.

✓

No false saddles

In our benchmark, ≈29 % of converged CI-NEB bands did not end on a first-order saddle.

Every reported saddle has exactly one negative Hessian eigenvalue (exact analytic Hessian on the default potential, noise-calibrated finite differences on the others) and is validated by downhill runs on both sides: one back to your initial state, one to a product minimum.

⬡

Built for difficult environments

Steps, vacancies, single-atom alloys and zeolite pores trap generic optimizers.

Benchmarked on stepped fcc(221) and bcc(211), hcp(10-10), vacancies, single-atom alloys and 9 zeolite frameworks with up to 254 atoms. The engine for metals and for zeolites is chosen automatically.

⌘

No expert tuning

Every method has its own knobs, step sizes and failure modes.

One command with an intent: fast, complete, low-barrier, network or DFT hand-off. Or let your coding agent drive it: the workspace ships an AGENTS.md entry point.

How it was built

An AI research loop that invents saddle-search algorithms and has to prove them

A frontier orchestrator model proposed ideas, parallel agents implemented and tested them on one CPU core each, and a fixed protocol decided what survived. The algorithms act in the full 3N-dimensional Cartesian space of the system; no reaction coordinate is chosen by hand.

Integrate

20+ methods

Dimer and κ-dimer, Lanczos, Hessian eigenvector-following and P-RFO, Sella (RS-PRFO), Newton trajectories and constrained relaxed scans, AFIR-type forcing and (CI-)NEB in one engine, under one validator and one cost ledger.

Invent

306 experiments

389 research rounds. Each proposes a mathematical change (new constraint surfaces, curvature control, memory, engine routing) and implements it.

Verify

122 validated

Pre-registered tests: development sets for screening, held-out pools for one-shot confirmation, sealed sets opened only at grand finals. Failures are kept: 140 experiments were negative, 35 inconclusive.

Ship

75 in the engine

Validated algorithms go into one package, together with usability and safety work (117 merged changes). Default: relaxed scan → Sella on metals, dimer elsewhere → Hessian certificate.

Results

Same CPU time, more verified saddles

TF-B1, pre-registered before it ran: the 39 sealed reactions of our final test set, never used for tuning (27 reactions on metal steps, vacancies and single-atom alloys; 12 acid-site proton transfers in 9 zeolite frameworks; 30–254 atoms), 2 seeds, one Perlmutter CPU core per job, the same machine-learned potential with exact Hessians for every method, 780 jobs, all completed. A saddle counts only if the exact Hessian has exactly one negative eigenvalue; for the searches, both downhill sides must also connect. Every method has the same verifier and the same CPU accounting.

In other words

  • In exactly the CPU time ASE dimer spent on each case and seed, the default had found 445 verified saddles against the dimer's 101.
  • Without the product, Transition Foundry recovered the reference transition state about as often as CI-NEB did with it: 38 of 78 runs (49 %) for the default and 46 of 78 (59 %) for long-more-auto-81, against 21 of 39 (54 %) for CI-NEB. Each run also returned 6.3 verified saddles on average.
  • CI-NEB is quick when the product is known (median 9.4 CPU-s per case). Stopped at CI-NEB's CPU time on the same case, the default had found 68 verified saddles against CI-NEB's 52 (CI-NEB counted once per seed), but CI-NEB, holding the product, had hit the reference more often by then (42 vs 8).

CI-NEB answers a different question: one transition state for a product you already know. It was run leniently: a 25× looser force threshold (0.05 eV/Å), no connectivity check, and the better of two protocols as the baseline. In zeolites its comparison is too noisy to call (0.91–2.24×, every CI includes 1).

Use it

Hand it to your agent

Transition Foundry is built to be driven by a coding agent. Let the agent read the workspace, tell it the reaction you care about, and it picks the most efficient search that needs no final state, runs it and returns certified transition states.

  1. 1Give your agent the Transition Foundry workspace (access on request).
  2. 2Describe your system and the reaction you care about, or just provide the initial state.
  3. 3Get certified transition states: barriers, products, IS/TS/FS structures and DFT-ready inputs.
Prompt for your agent
Read AGENTS.md in the Transition Foundry workspace at <path>, then the documents it points to. My system: <catalyst / surface / zeolite, adsorbate>. Initial state: <IS.extxyz, or how to build it>. Reaction of interest: <e.g. C–H scission of CH3OH, proton transfer from the acid site>. Force field: <default, or mace-mp / chgnet / sevennet / mattersim / orb / my own ASE calculator>. Choose the most efficient preset that needs no final state for this goal, check the installation and the force field with `dimer-explore doctor`, run the search on a compute node, and report every certified transition state with its barrier, reaction energy and product, plus the IS/TS/FS structure files. Flag anything uncertain.
Or run it yourself
pip install -e '/path/to/dimer-exploration[hessian,sella]'
# model, imports, memory and time estimate
dimer-explore doctor --structure IS.extxyz
# default: fast, no final state
dimer-explore explore IS.extxyz --out run
# more of the mechanism
dimer-explore explore IS.extxyz --goal complete --out run2
# the same search on MACE-MP-0 (pip install mace-torch)
dimer-explore explore IS.extxyz --calculator mace-mp --out run3
# 3-D IS/TS/FS viewer
dimer-explore view run

Honest limits

What it does not claim

Machine-learned energies

Barriers come from machine-learned potentials: use them to rank and screen, and confirm with DFT. The speed-ups above were measured on the default potential with exact Hessians; the other force fields are verified end to end, not re-benchmarked. Savings for direct DFT searches have not been demonstrated yet.

Efficient, not exhaustive

On 85 development states an exhaustive census found 1,296 strict saddles; the default's two-seed union covered about 490. Use complete or network for broader coverage.

CI-NEB is not obsolete

If you already know the product and want that single path, CI-NEB is fast and precise enough. Transition Foundry is for when you do not know where the reaction goes.

Scope of the tests

Metal surfaces (steps, vacancies, single-atom alloys) and zeolites, 30–254 atoms; neutral, spin-free systems.
